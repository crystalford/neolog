A finished essay no longer proves anyone thought it. The road to it does — and the log already keeps the road.

Anyone can produce a good essay now. So "this is good" stops telling anyone that you thought it. What can't be produced on demand is what came before: the sentence said in the car, the same idea back three weeks later, the day something happened that confirmed it, the revision. You can fake an essay. You can't fake three months of a thought coming back to you — that takes living three months. The log stores that part. Everyone else stores the finished thing.

4not this

The proof is the dates. It is not a count of how often you said it.

Worth saying plainly, because it's an easy slide. The log doesn't decide a thought matters because it came back five times, and it doesn't tell you so. What it does is quieter and more useful: when you decide something matters, it can show — to the day — when you had it, what it was made of, and how it changed.

Two things that look alike and aren't.

"You've circled this five times in three months" is the log commenting on you. You know you've said it before; being told is the thing you asked it never to do. "First said 19 August, revised 3 September, two events behind it" is the log answering a question you asked. Same data, opposite direction. The first is a nudge. The second is a receipt. Only the second is built.

The quiet form is allowed: an old entry shown again on its anniversary, with no comment, the way a diary falls open at a page. That's on this day, already in the rail. It shows; it doesn't say.

The dates are the proof.

Not the finished piece — anyone can have one of those now. The first time, the return, the revision, the events between, each with a day on it. The log keeps those as a matter of course; export turns them into evidence.

Who wrote each line is a field.

Evidence, not a nudge.

The log never tells you a thought matters because it recurred. When you decide it matters, the log can prove when you had it. The difference is who asked.
